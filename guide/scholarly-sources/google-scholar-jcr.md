# Google Scholar & Verifying Journal Quality

Finding a relevant article is only half the job — you also need to know whether the journal it appeared in is credible.
Google Scholar is the fastest way to cast a wide net across scholarly literature, but it doesn't filter by quality the way IEEE Xplore or Web of Science does.
This page shows you how to set up Google Scholar for full-text access through the U of I Library, and then how to use Journal Citation Reports and journal websites to verify that the sources you find are worth citing in your paper.

## What Google Scholar Searches (and What It Doesn't)

[Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/) indexes articles, theses, conference papers, preprints, and technical reports from publishers, university repositories, and the open web.
Its biggest strength is **breadth**: a single search can surface results from IEEE, Springer, Elsevier, ACM, arXiv, and thousands of other sources at once.
It's also fast and familiar — the interface works just like a regular Google search.

That breadth comes with trade-offs, though.
Google Scholar doesn't let you filter by document type, subject category, or database the way IEEE Xplore and Web of Science do.
It can return preprints that haven't been peer-reviewed, student theses, and low-quality articles from **predatory journals** (publications that charge fees to publish papers with little or no legitimate peer review).
Google Scholar also doesn't provide citation-tracking tools as detailed as Web of Science's.

The practical takeaway: Google Scholar is a great starting point for discovery, especially when your topic spans multiple fields, but you should always verify journal quality before citing what you find.
The rest of this page shows you how.

## Setting Up Library Integration

By default, Google Scholar shows you article metadata — titles, authors, abstracts — but links to the full text often land on a publisher paywall.
You can fix this by telling Google Scholar about your U of I Library affiliation so that full-text links appear directly in your search results.

1. Go to [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/) and click the hamburger menu (three horizontal lines) in the upper-left corner, then select **Settings**.
2. In the left sidebar, click **Library links**.
3. In the search box, type **University of Idaho** and press the search button.
4. Check the box next to **University of Idaho** when it appears in the results.
5. Click **Save**.

After saving, your Google Scholar results will include a "FindIt@UI" or "Full Text @ U of I" link on the right side of each result whenever the library has access.
Clicking that link routes you through the library proxy so you can download the PDF at no cost.

## Introduction to Journal Citation Reports (JCR)

Once you've found an article through Google Scholar (or any other source), how do you know whether the journal it appeared in is respected in the field?
**Journal Citation Reports (JCR)** is a tool published by Clarivate that measures journal influence using citation data.

JCR's best-known metric is the **Journal Impact Factor (JIF)**, which reflects the average number of citations received per article published in that journal over the previous two years.
A higher impact factor generally means the journal's articles are cited more often, which is one indicator (though not the only one) that the research community considers the journal credible and influential.

JCR is a subscription resource.
Access it through the U of I Library so the proxy handles authentication automatically.

## Checking Impact Factor and Category Ranking

Here's how to use JCR to evaluate a journal:

1. **Search for the journal by name.**
Enter the journal title (for example, *IEEE Transactions on Signal Processing*) in the JCR search bar.
2. **Review the Journal Impact Factor.**
The journal's profile page displays its current JIF along with trends over recent years.
A rising or stable impact factor suggests the journal maintains consistent quality.
3. **Check the category ranking.**
JCR assigns each journal to one or more subject categories (e.g., "Engineering, Electrical & Electronic") and ranks it within that category by quartile.
A journal ranked in **Q1** (top 25%) is among the most-cited in its field; **Q2** is above the median.
For a two-page ECE research paper, citing articles from Q1 or Q2 journals is a strong sign of source quality.

If you can't find a journal in JCR, that doesn't automatically mean the journal is low quality — some newer or more specialized journals simply aren't indexed yet.
But it does mean you should look more carefully before citing it.

## Verifying Peer Review on a Journal's Website

JCR isn't the only way to assess a journal.
You can also go directly to the journal's own website and look for evidence that it uses a genuine **peer-review process** (a system where submitted manuscripts are evaluated by independent experts before publication).

Look for these indicators:

- **Editorial board** — A legitimate journal lists its editors and editorial board members, who are typically established researchers with university or institutional affiliations.
- **Submission guidelines** — Reputable journals publish detailed instructions for authors that describe the review process, expected timelines, and manuscript formatting requirements.
- **Review process description** — Look for language like "double-blind peer review" or "single-blind review," which describes how the journal evaluates submissions.
- **Indexing information** — Journals often note which databases index them (e.g., Web of Science, Scopus, IEEE Xplore). Being indexed in these databases is a positive quality signal.

If a journal's website is vague about its review process, lists no recognizable editorial board members, or prominently advertises rapid publication with guaranteed acceptance, treat it as a red flag.
These are common characteristics of predatory journals.

## A Note on Impact Factor as One Indicator

The Journal Impact Factor is widely used, but it has limitations.
It reflects average citation rates across an entire journal, not the quality of any individual article.
Some excellent papers appear in lower-impact journals, and some highly cited papers are cited because they're controversial rather than because they're good.

For your assignment, impact factor and JCR quartile ranking are useful, practical tools for quickly gauging whether a journal is credible.
But they work best when combined with the other checks described above — looking at the editorial board, confirming peer review, and reading the article itself critically.
