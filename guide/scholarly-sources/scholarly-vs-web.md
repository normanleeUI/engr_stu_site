# Scholarly vs. Web Sources

When you start researching a topic for your two-page IEEE-format paper, you'll find information everywhere — blog posts, news articles, Wikipedia, YouTube videos, and academic journal articles.
Not all of these carry the same weight in an engineering paper.
Understanding the difference between scholarly and general web sources is one of the most important skills you'll use in this assignment, because the sources you cite directly affect your paper's credibility.

## Why the Distinction Matters

Your professors expect you to ground your arguments in **peer-reviewed research** — work that has been evaluated by other experts in the field before publication.
When you cite a peer-reviewed source, you're telling your reader: "This claim is backed by evidence that independent experts have vetted."

General web sources like blog posts, news articles, and Wikipedia entries don't go through this vetting process.
They can be useful for getting oriented on a topic, but they don't carry the same authority.
If your paper's reference list is filled with web sources instead of scholarly ones, it signals that your research stayed at the surface level.

For your assignment, you're expected to cite sources from organizations like **IEEE** (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers), **ASEE** (American Society for Engineering Education), **ASME** (American Society of Mechanical Engineers), or similar professional and academic institutions.
These organizations publish rigorously reviewed research that meets the standard your instructor is looking for.

## What Makes a Source "Scholarly"

A **scholarly source** (also called an academic or peer-reviewed source) is a publication written by researchers or subject-matter experts, reviewed by other experts before publication, and intended for an academic audience.
Here are the key characteristics:

- **Peer review** — Before publication, the manuscript is sent to independent experts who evaluate the methodology, evidence, and conclusions.
This process catches errors, weak arguments, and unsupported claims before they reach print.

- **Methodology and evidence** — Scholarly articles describe how the research was conducted in enough detail that another researcher could replicate it.
They present data, analysis, and results rather than opinions.

- **Institutional affiliation** — Authors are typically affiliated with a university, research lab, or professional organization.
Their credentials and affiliations are listed on the article.

- **References** — Scholarly articles cite the prior work they build on.
A references section at the end lets you trace the evidence chain back to its origins.

- **Specialized audience** — The writing assumes familiarity with the field's terminology and methods.
It isn't written to entertain a general audience.

Not every scholarly source is a journal article.
Conference papers (very common in ECE), technical reports, and theses can also be scholarly, as long as they've gone through a formal review process.

## A Concrete Example: Comparing Sources on the Same Topic

Imagine you're writing about advances in autonomous vehicle sensor systems.
Here's what a scholarly source and a web source on that topic might look like:

### Scholarly journal article

- **Title:** "LiDAR-Camera Fusion for Real-Time Object Detection in Autonomous Driving"
- **Authors:** Three researchers with Ph.D. affiliations at university labs
- **Published in:** *IEEE Transactions on Intelligent Transportation Systems*
- **Structure:** Abstract, introduction, related work, methodology, experimental results, conclusion, and a list of 30+ references
- **How to find it:** Through [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/) or a library database

This article describes a specific algorithm, tests it against benchmarks, reports quantitative results, and has been reviewed by other researchers before publication.
You can cite this with confidence.

### Blog post on the same topic

- **Title:** "How Self-Driving Cars See the World"
- **Author:** A technology journalist (no listed credentials in sensor engineering)
- **Published on:** A popular tech blog
- **Structure:** A narrative overview with embedded stock photos, no methodology section, and no references
- **How to find it:** A Google search

This post might give you a helpful overview, and it might even be accurate.
But it hasn't been vetted by experts, it doesn't show its evidence, and it isn't considered a credible source in an academic paper.

### Wikipedia entry

Wikipedia is a useful starting point for understanding a topic's basics.
However, Wikipedia articles are written and edited by anonymous volunteers, can change at any time, and are not peer-reviewed.
Your instructor will not accept Wikipedia as a cited source.

That said, Wikipedia can be genuinely helpful in one way: scroll to the bottom of a Wikipedia article and look at its references.
Those references often point to the scholarly articles you actually want to cite.

## How to Recognize a Scholarly Source

When you find an article, ask yourself these questions:

1. **Who wrote it?**
Look for author names with institutional affiliations (e.g., "Department of Electrical Engineering, University of Illinois").
If the author is anonymous or is a journalist rather than a researcher, it's probably not scholarly.

2. **Where was it published?**
Scholarly journals and conference proceedings have names like *IEEE Transactions on Power Systems*, *Proceedings of the ASME International Mechanical Engineering Congress*, or *Journal of Engineering Education*.
If it was published on a .com blog or a news site, it's not scholarly.

3. **Does it have a DOI?**
A **DOI** (Digital Object Identifier) is a permanent link assigned to published works.
Most scholarly articles have one (e.g., `doi:10.1109/TITS.2024.1234567`).
A DOI doesn't guarantee a source is scholarly, but its absence from a journal article is a warning sign.

4. **Is there a references section?**
Scholarly work always cites its sources.
If the article has no bibliography or works-cited list, it hasn't engaged with the existing research.

5. **Does it describe a methodology?**
Scholarly articles explain how the research was done — what data was collected, what experiments were run, what analytical framework was used.
If the piece just states conclusions without showing the work, it isn't scholarly.

## A Note on "Gray Area" Sources

Some sources fall between clearly scholarly and clearly non-scholarly:

- **Industry white papers** (e.g., from Intel or Texas Instruments) — These can contain solid technical content but haven't been peer-reviewed.
Ask your instructor before citing them.

- **Government technical reports** (e.g., from NIST or DOE) — These often undergo internal review and can be acceptable in engineering papers.
Check your assignment guidelines before relying on them.

- **Preprints** (e.g., from arXiv) — These are research papers posted before peer review.
They may contain good work, but they haven't been vetted yet.
Some instructors accept them; others don't.

When in doubt, ask your instructor or check with a [librarian at the University of Idaho Library](https://www.lib.uidaho.edu/help/).
