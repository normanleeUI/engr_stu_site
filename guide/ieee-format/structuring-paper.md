# Structuring Your Paper

An IEEE-format paper follows a predictable structure that readers in engineering and computer science already know how to navigate.
When you use that structure, your reader spends less effort figuring out where things are and more effort engaging with your ideas.
This page walks through each standard section so you can plan your two-page paper before you start drafting.

## Why Structure Matters

IEEE papers use a conventional sequence of sections — Title, Abstract, Introduction, Body, Conclusion, References — because it mirrors how engineers think through a problem: context, contribution, evidence, and implications.
Reviewers and readers expect this order.
Deviating from it does not signal creativity; it signals that the author is unfamiliar with the format.

For a two-page paper, space is tight.
Every section needs to earn its place, and you should plan approximate lengths before you write so that no single section crowds out the others.

## Title and Abstract

Your **title** should be specific enough that a reader can tell what your paper covers without opening it.
A title like "Machine Learning in Power Systems" is too broad; "Fault Detection in Distribution Networks Using Random Forest Classification" tells the reader exactly what to expect.

The **abstract** is a self-contained summary of your entire paper — the problem, your approach, and your key finding — written in a single paragraph.
For a two-page paper, aim for 50–100 words.
Write the abstract last, after you know what the paper actually says.

## Introduction

The Introduction does three things in roughly this order:

1. **Establishes context.**
   Describe the broader topic or problem area so the reader understands why the work matters.
2. **States the specific problem or gap.**
   Narrow from the general context to the particular question your paper addresses.
3. **Previews your contribution.**
   Briefly state what your paper does — whether it proposes a solution, surveys existing research, or explores a topic in depth.

In a two-page paper, the Introduction typically runs three to four paragraphs — roughly one-quarter to one-third of your available space.
Resist the urge to spend most of the paper on background.
The Introduction sets up the problem; the body sections are where you deliver on the promise.

## Body Sections

The body is where you present your research, analysis, or calculations.
This is the core of your paper, and it should occupy roughly half of your two pages.

Divide the body into sections with descriptive headings.
The exact number and names depend on your topic, but common patterns include:

- **Literature review or background**, summarizing the most relevant prior work
- **Methodology or approach**, describing what you did or how you analyzed the problem
- **Results or analysis**, presenting your findings with supporting evidence

Each claim you make in the body must be supported.
Your assignment states that you must "cite all sources and support all statements with research or calculations."
This means every factual claim needs either a citation to a published source or your own data and calculations to back it up.
Unsupported assertions — even ones that seem obvious — weaken your paper.

Use IEEE-style numbered citations (e.g., [1], [2]) to reference your sources inline.
When you present data or calculations, explain what they show and why they matter rather than letting a table or equation speak for itself.

## Conclusion

The Conclusion serves three purposes:

1. **Summarize your findings.**
   Restate the key results or arguments from the body, but do not simply repeat sentences from earlier sections.
2. **State the implications.**
   Explain what your findings mean for the broader field, application, or problem area.
3. **Note limitations or future work.**
   Briefly acknowledge what your paper does not cover or what further research could address.

For a two-page paper, the Conclusion is typically one to two paragraphs.
Keep it concise — the reader has just finished your analysis and does not need a lengthy recap.

## References

The References section lists every source you cited in the paper, numbered in the order they first appear in your text.
This section does not count toward your two-page limit in most IEEE templates, but check your assignment instructions to confirm.

A two-page research paper typically cites between five and fifteen sources, depending on the topic.
Quality matters more than quantity — choose sources that directly support your claims.

## Planning Your Space

With only two pages, planning how much space each section gets prevents the common mistake of writing a long introduction and running out of room for analysis.
Here is a rough guide:

| Section | Approximate Length |
|---|---|
| Abstract | 50–100 words |
| Introduction | 3–4 paragraphs (¼ to ⅓ of the paper) |
| Body sections | ~½ of the paper |
| Conclusion | 1–2 paragraphs |
| References | Typically separate (check your assignment) |

These are guidelines, not rules.
The right balance depends on your topic and how much evidence you need to present.
But if your Introduction is longer than your body, or your Conclusion introduces new information, those are signs that something needs restructuring.
