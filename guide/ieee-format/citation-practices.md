# IEEE Citation Practices

Every claim in your paper needs a citation, and IEEE has a specific way of handling them.
Unlike APA or MLA — which use author-name citations — IEEE uses a numbered system with square brackets that keeps your text compact and readable.
Understanding how this system works will help you cite sources correctly in your two-page paper and avoid the formatting mistakes that are easiest to make.

## How IEEE Citations Work

IEEE uses a **numbered citation system**.
Each source you cite gets a number in square brackets — [1], [2], [3] — and sources are numbered **in the order they first appear in your text**, not alphabetically.
The first source you mention is [1], the second is [2], and so on.

When you refer to a source again later in the paper, you reuse its original number.
If you first cite a source in your Introduction as [3], it stays [3] every time you reference it, even in your Conclusion.
You can also cite multiple sources in a single bracket: [1], [3] or [1]–[3] for a continuous range.

Place the citation number inside the sentence as a noun or at the end of the clause it supports.
For example:

- "As shown in [4], convolutional neural networks outperform traditional classifiers in image recognition tasks."
- "Recent studies have demonstrated improved fault detection rates using machine learning [5], [7]."

Notice that the citation number appears before the period, not after it.

## Why IEEE Uses This System

The numbered citation format exists for practical reasons.
Engineering papers — especially conference papers — have strict page limits.
Writing "[1]" takes far less space than "(Smith et al., 2024)," and that difference adds up across a two-page paper with ten or more citations.

Numbering by order of appearance also reflects your paper's narrative flow.
A reader encounters [1] before [2] because that is the order in which you build your argument.
This makes it easy to follow along: if a reader sees [7] in your Conclusion, they know it was introduced later in the paper than [2], which appeared in your Introduction.

## Formatting Your Reference List

Your reference list appears at the end of the paper under the heading "References."
Each entry is numbered to match the in-text citations, and entries appear in numerical order (the order you first cited them), not alphabetically.

The exact format for each entry depends on the source type.
Here are the most common types you will cite in your paper, with the required fields for each.

### Journal Articles

Include the author(s), article title in quotation marks, journal title in italics (abbreviated per IEEE conventions), volume, number, page range, month, and year.

> [1] A. B. Smith and C. D. Jones, "Fault detection in smart grids using deep learning," *IEEE Trans. Power Syst.*, vol. 38, no. 4, pp. 3201–3210, Jul. 2023.

### Conference Papers

Include the author(s), paper title in quotation marks, conference name in italics (abbreviated), location, month, year, and page range.

> [2] E. F. Garcia, "Real-time signal processing on FPGA platforms," in *Proc. IEEE Int. Conf. Acoust., Speech Signal Process. (ICASSP)*, Rhodes, Greece, Jun. 2023, pp. 1045–1049.

### Books

Include the author(s), book title in italics, edition (if not the first), publisher, city, and year.

> [3] S. Haykin, *Neural Networks and Learning Machines*, 3rd ed. Upper Saddle River, NJ, USA: Pearson, 2009.

### Online Sources

Include the author (or organization), title in quotation marks, website name in italics, publication date if available, URL, and the access date.

> [4] National Institute of Standards and Technology, "Cybersecurity framework," *NIST*, Feb. 2024. [Online]. Available: https://www.nist.gov/cyberframework. [Accessed: Jun. 5, 2026].

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

These are the citation errors that show up most often in student papers:

- **Alphabetical ordering.**
Listing references alphabetically is an APA and MLA convention.
In IEEE, the reference list must follow order of first appearance.
If your references are in alphabetical order, your citation numbers will appear out of sequence in the text, and the paper will not conform to IEEE format.

- **Inconsistent formatting.**
Mixing styles within the reference list — some entries with full journal names, others abbreviated; some with quotation marks around titles, others without — makes the paper look careless.
Pick one format and apply it consistently to every entry.
The IEEE Reference Guide provides the standard format for each source type.

- **Missing required fields.**
Every reference type has a set of required fields.
A journal article without a volume and page range, or a conference paper without a location, is incomplete.
Before submitting, check each entry against the IEEE Reference Guide to make sure nothing is missing.

- **Citing sources you did not read.**
If you found a claim in Source A that attributes it to Source B, cite Source A (the source you actually read) unless you go back and read Source B yourself.
Citing sources secondhand without reading them can lead to misrepresentation.

## The IEEE Editorial Style Manual

The [IEEE Editorial Style Manual](https://journals.ieeeauthorcenter.ieee.org/your-role-in-article-production/ieee-editorial-style-manual/) is the official reference for all IEEE publication standards, covering everything from abbreviation conventions to mathematical notation.
For citation formatting specifically, the **IEEE Reference Guide** — linked from the Style Manual page — is the most useful document.
It provides detailed templates and examples for every source type IEEE recognizes.

You do not need to memorize the Style Manual, but knowing where to find it means you can quickly check a formatting question instead of guessing.
Bookmark it and refer to it when you are assembling your reference list.
