# Citation Chaining

Once you have found one good source for your paper, you can use it to find more.
**Citation chaining** is a research technique where you follow the connections between papers — either looking at what a paper cites or finding what has cited it — to discover related work.
It is one of the most efficient ways to build a focused set of sources because every link you follow is already connected to your topic.

## Backward Chaining

**Backward chaining** means looking at a paper's reference list to find its sources.
When you read a useful paper, the authors have already done the work of identifying the foundational research that supports their findings.
Their reference list is essentially a curated reading list for that topic.

This technique is especially useful for finding the seminal work in a topic area — the original studies, key datasets, or foundational methods that later researchers built upon.
For a two-page IEEE paper, backward chaining helps you quickly identify the landmark references that give your paper a solid evidentiary foundation.

To use backward chaining, scroll to the references section of a paper you've already found useful.
Look for titles that relate directly to the specific aspect of the topic you're writing about, then retrieve those papers and evaluate them as potential sources for your own work.

## Forward Chaining

**Forward chaining** is the reverse: you start with a known paper and find newer papers that cite it.
This shows you how a topic has evolved since that paper was published — who extended its methods, who challenged its conclusions, and what new directions emerged from it.

Forward chaining is particularly valuable when your starting paper is a few years old.
The research that cites it will be more recent and may reflect advances in tools, techniques, or understanding that are relevant to your paper.
It also helps you find papers that address limitations in the original study, which can strengthen the way you discuss the validity of your own sources.

## How to Chain in IEEE Xplore

[IEEE Xplore](PROXY_URL_PLACEHOLDER) is one of the primary databases for ECE research, and it supports both directions of citation chaining directly from any article page.

**For backward chaining**, open the article you want to chain from and look for the **References** tab on the article page.
This tab lists every source the paper cited.
Each reference includes a link to the full record if it is available in IEEE Xplore, so you can quickly open and evaluate potential sources.

**For forward chaining**, look for the **Cited By** section on the same article page.
This section shows papers in the IEEE Xplore database that have cited your starting paper.
The list is sorted by date, so the most recent citing papers appear first.

## How to Chain in Web of Science

[Web of Science](PROXY_URL_PLACEHOLDER) covers a broader range of journals and conferences than IEEE Xplore alone, which makes it useful for finding citing papers from adjacent fields.

**For backward chaining**, open an article record and click the **Cited References** link.
This shows the full list of sources the paper used, with links to their records in Web of Science where available.

**For forward chaining**, click the **Times Cited** count on the article record.
This takes you to a list of every indexed paper that has cited your starting paper.
You can sort and filter the results by date, subject area, or document type to narrow the list to the most relevant papers.

## How to Chain in Google Scholar

[Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/) is freely available and covers the widest range of sources, including preprints, theses, and conference papers that may not appear in IEEE Xplore or Web of Science.

**For backward chaining**, Google Scholar does not display a paper's full reference list directly.
Instead, click through to the paper itself (the PDF or publisher page) and consult the references section in the document.

**For forward chaining**, look for the **Cited by** link below any search result.
Clicking it shows a list of papers that cite your starting paper.
This is one of the fastest ways to find newer work on a topic, because Google Scholar indexes sources broadly and updates frequently.

Keep in mind that Google Scholar does not filter for peer review.
Some of the citing papers may be theses, technical reports, or non-peer-reviewed preprints.
Always check the publication venue before deciding to use a citing paper as a source for your assignment.

## Putting It Together

A practical approach for your two-page IEEE paper is to combine both directions.
Start with one strong paper on your topic.
Chain backward to find the foundational work it builds on, then chain forward to find recent papers that extend or refine its findings.
Two or three rounds of chaining from a single strong starting paper can give you a well-rounded set of sources — older foundational references and newer developments — without having to run dozens of keyword searches.
