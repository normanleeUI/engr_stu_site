# AI Search Tools & Their Limitations

AI tools can feel like a shortcut to finding sources for your paper — type a question, get a list of references in seconds.
But the sources AI gives you may not exist at all.
Understanding how these tools actually work, and where they fail, is essential before you rely on any AI-generated citation in your two-page IEEE-format paper.

## How Different AI Tools Find Information

Not all AI tools search the same way, and the differences matter for the quality of the sources they return.

**ChatGPT** (and similar large language models like Claude or Gemini) does not search a database when you ask it for references.
It generates text based on patterns learned during training.
When you ask ChatGPT for "three IEEE papers on FPGA-based signal processing," it predicts what a plausible-sounding citation looks like — real author names, realistic journal titles, properly formatted DOIs — but it is not looking up actual papers.
The result can be entirely fabricated.

**Perplexity AI** takes a different approach.
It searches the open web in real time and attempts to cite the pages it finds.
This is more grounded than ChatGPT's approach because the sources usually exist, but "exists on the web" is not the same as "is a peer-reviewed scholarly source."
Perplexity may return blog posts, press releases, or preprints alongside actual journal articles without distinguishing between them.

**Semantic Scholar** is an academic search engine that uses AI features to help you explore the research literature.
Unlike ChatGPT, it queries an actual database of published papers — over 200 million of them.
Its AI-powered features, like TLDR summaries and research recommendations, are built on top of real bibliographic records.
This makes it significantly more reliable than a general-purpose chatbot, but it is still not perfect.

**Google Scholar** is not an AI tool in the same sense, but students often compare it to the others.
Google Scholar indexes real scholarly articles from publisher websites, institutional repositories, and preprint servers.
It does not generate or fabricate results, though it does index some non-peer-reviewed content like theses, reports, and preprints.

The key difference is what sits behind each tool: a language model generating plausible text, a web crawler pulling live pages, or an academic database of indexed publications.
The closer the tool is to an actual database of verified records, the more trustworthy its results — but none of them eliminate the need for you to verify what you find.

## Why AI-Generated Citations Must Always Be Verified

Large language models like ChatGPT do not retrieve information — they generate it.
When you ask for a citation, the model constructs something that looks like a real reference because it has seen millions of real references during training.
This is called **hallucination**: the model produces output that is confident, detailed, and wrong.

A hallucinated citation might include a real author's name paired with a paper they never wrote, a journal title that exists but never published that article, or a DOI that follows the correct format but resolves to nothing.
Every element looks legitimate on its own, which makes hallucinated citations especially dangerous — you won't notice the problem unless you check.

This is not a rare edge case.
Studies have found that a significant percentage of citations generated by large language models are partially or completely fabricated.
The models are not trying to deceive you; they simply have no mechanism for confirming whether the text they generate corresponds to a real publication.

## A Concrete Example: When AI Gets It Wrong

Imagine you ask an AI assistant: "Give me a recent IEEE paper on reinforcement learning for robotic arm control."

The tool responds with something like:

> J. Chen, M. Patel, and R. Liu, "Adaptive Deep Reinforcement Learning for Multi-Joint Robotic Manipulation," *IEEE Transactions on Robotics*, vol. 41, no. 3, pp. 1245–1260, Jun. 2025, doi: 10.1109/TRO.2025.3298471.

This looks perfect.
The author names are common in the field.
*IEEE Transactions on Robotics* is a real, well-known journal.
The DOI follows IEEE's formatting conventions.
The volume, issue, and page numbers all look plausible.

But when you paste that DOI into a browser or search for the title in [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/), nothing comes up.
The paper does not exist.
The AI assembled a realistic-looking citation from patterns in its training data without checking whether any of it was real.

This problem is not limited to general-purpose chatbots.
Even tools that query real databases can return misleading results.
Semantic Scholar's AI recommendation features might surface a paper with a slightly garbled title or misattributed author list if the underlying metadata in its index contains errors.
Perplexity might cite a retracted paper or a predatory journal article because it found it on the web and had no way to evaluate its quality.

The lesson is the same regardless of the tool: **an AI-generated citation is a suggestion, not a fact**.

## AI as a Discovery Tool vs. AI as a Citation Source

There is an important distinction between using AI to explore a topic and using AI to generate your reference list.

As a **discovery tool**, AI can be genuinely useful.
You can ask a chatbot to explain a concept, suggest search terms, or outline the key debates in a research area.
This can help you figure out what to search for when you sit down with a real database like IEEE Xplore or Web of Science.
Think of it the same way you might use Wikipedia — helpful for orientation, not appropriate to cite.

As a **citation source**, AI is unreliable.
If you copy citations directly from ChatGPT, Perplexity, or any other AI tool into your reference list without verifying them, you risk citing papers that don't exist, misattributing ideas to the wrong authors, or referencing articles from predatory journals that aren't peer-reviewed.
Any of these would damage your paper's credibility and could violate your course's academic integrity policies.

The practical rule is simple: use AI to help you figure out *what* to search for, then use a library database to find and verify the actual sources you cite.

## How to Verify an AI-Suggested Source

If an AI tool suggests a source that looks promising, take these steps before adding it to your paper:

1. **Search for the exact title** in [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/), [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/), or [Web of Science](PROXY_URL_PLACEHOLDER).
If the paper exists, you should be able to find it by its title.

2. **Check the DOI.**
Copy the DOI and paste it into [https://doi.org/](https://doi.org/) followed by the DOI string.
If it resolves to a real publication page, the DOI is valid.
If you get a "DOI not found" error, the citation is fabricated.

3. **Confirm the authors and publication details.**
Even if a paper with a similar title exists, verify that the authors, journal name, volume, and year match what the AI gave you.
AI tools sometimes combine real elements from different papers into a single fictional citation.

4. **Access the full text.**
Read at least the abstract and conclusions to confirm the paper is actually relevant to your argument.
A real paper with the right title can still be off-topic for your specific claim.

5. **Cite from the database, not from the AI.**
Once you've confirmed the source is real, pull the citation information from the database record itself — not from what the AI originally generated.
IEEE Xplore and Google Scholar both offer export options that give you a correctly formatted reference.
