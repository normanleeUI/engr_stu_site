# AI Search Tools and Their Limitations

AI tools can feel like a shortcut to finding sources for your paper — type a question, get a list of references in seconds.
But the sources AI gives you may not exist or mat not be peer reviewed.
Understanding how these tools actually work, and where they fail, is essential before you rely on AI tools.

## How Different AI Tools Find Information

Not all AI tools search the same way, and the differences matter for the quality of the sources they return.

**ChatGPT, Perplexiy AI, and similar tools** do not search academic databases when you ask it for references. It searches the web, similar to Google, and then tries to answer your question. Importantly, if it doesn't find an answer on the web, it often makes things up instead of telling you it failed. If it does find an answer, the sources many not be peer reviewed.

**Semantic Scholar and Google Scholar Labs** are academic search engines that uses AI features to help you explore the research literature.
Unlike ChatGPT, they query actual databases of papers. However, similar to basic Google Scholar, not all of these will be peer reviewed.

## AI as a Discovery Tool vs. AI as a Citation Source

There is an important distinction between using AI to explore a topic and using AI to generate your reference list.

As a **discovery tool**, AI can be genuinely useful.
You can ask a chatbot to explain a concept, suggest search terms, or outline the key debates in a research area.
This can help you figure out what to search for when you sit down with a real database like IEEE Xplore or Web of Science.

As a **citation source**, AI is unreliable.
If you copy citations directly from ChatGPT, Perplexity, or any other AI tool into your reference list without verifying them, you risk citing papers that don't exist, misattributing ideas to the wrong authors, or referencing articles from predatory journals that aren't peer-reviewed.

## How to Verify an AI-Suggested Source

If an AI tool suggests a source that looks promising, take these steps before adding it to your paper:

1. **Confirm the source exists.**
Search for the exact title in [IEEE Xplore](https://uidaho.idm.oclc.org/login?url=https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/), [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/), or [Web of Science](https://uidaho.idm.oclc.org/login?url=https://www.webofscience.com/wos/woscc/basic-search).
If you cannot find it anywhere, the citation may be fabricated — do not use it.

2. **Check that it is peer-reviewed.**
Confirm the source appears in a peer-reviewed journal or conference proceedings, not just on a preprint server or personal website.

3. **Read before you cite.**
Read the piece yourself to confirm it actually says what AI says it says.

4. **Cite from the database, not from the AI.**
Once you have confirmed the source is real and peer-reviewed, pull the citation information from the database record itself — not from what the AI originally generated.
IEEE Xplore and Google Scholar both offer export options that give you a correctly formatted reference.
