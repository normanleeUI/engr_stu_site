# Appropriate vs. Inappropriate AI Use

AI tools like ChatGPT, Copilot, and Claude can be genuinely useful when you're writing a research paper — but only if you use them in ways that keep *you* in control of the thinking.
The line between appropriate and inappropriate use isn't about which tool you use or how often.
It's about whether the final product reflects your understanding or the AI's.
This page walks through concrete examples so you can make good decisions as you work on your paper.

## The General Principle

Here's the core idea: **AI should assist your work, not replace your thinking.**

Your assignment is evaluating whether *you* can research a topic, synthesize information from multiple sources, and communicate what you've learned in writing.
If an AI does the synthesizing or the writing for you, your paper may look polished, but it won't demonstrate that you've learned anything.
The distinction matters because the skills this assignment builds — finding evidence, evaluating it critically, and explaining it clearly — are skills you'll use throughout your engineering career.

A useful test: if you removed the AI from your workflow, would you still understand everything in your paper well enough to explain it in a conversation?
If yes, you probably used AI appropriately.
If not, the AI did too much.

## The Flow Diagram Example

Your professor has given a specific example that illustrates the line clearly.

- **Appropriate:** You hand-sketch a flow diagram that shows your understanding of a process or system.
Then you ask an AI tool to clean up the lines, improve the layout, and make the diagram look presentable.
The intellectual work — deciding what the diagram should contain, how the components connect, what the flow represents — is yours.
The AI just made it look better.

- **Inappropriate:** You give an AI your code or your topic and ask it to generate a flow diagram for you.
The AI decides what to include, how to organize it, and what relationships to show.
Even if the result is accurate, you haven't demonstrated that you understand the system.
You've outsourced the thinking.

The difference isn't cosmetic.
In the first case, you did the engineering work and used AI as a drafting tool.
In the second case, the AI did the engineering work and you submitted it.

## Examples for Your Research Paper

The same principle applies to the writing process.
Here are examples you're likely to encounter while working on your two-page IEEE-format paper.

### Grammar and Clarity

- **Appropriate:** You've written a sentence and it feels awkward, so you ask an AI to suggest a clearer way to phrase it.
You review the suggestion, decide whether it says what you meant, and either adopt it or revise further.
This is similar to asking a friend to proofread a paragraph.

- **Inappropriate:** You ask an AI to write a paragraph or an entire section of your paper.
Even if you edit the result afterward, the structure, logic, and argument originated with the AI, not with you.

### Understanding Concepts

- **Appropriate:** You're struggling to understand a concept from one of your sources — maybe a specific modulation technique or a power management strategy.
You ask an AI to explain it in simpler terms.
Then you write about that concept in your own words, based on your new understanding, and cite the original source.

- **Inappropriate:** You paste an AI-generated explanation directly into your paper, with or without minor edits.
The words and framing aren't yours, and your paper doesn't reflect your own process of understanding the material.

### Working with Sources

- **Appropriate:** You ask an AI to help you understand the structure of an IEEE citation so you can format your references correctly.
You're learning a skill.

- **Inappropriate:** You ask an AI to find sources for your paper and summarize them.
You haven't done the research yourself, and you can't verify that the sources are real, accurate, or relevant without doing the work the AI was supposed to save you.

## Why This Matters

It's tempting to think of AI restrictions as arbitrary rules.
They aren't.
Your professor assigns a two-page research paper because the process of writing it teaches you things that reading alone doesn't.

When you search for sources, you learn how knowledge is organized in your field.
When you evaluate those sources, you develop judgment about what constitutes good evidence.
When you write about what you found, you discover gaps in your own understanding — places where you thought you knew something but can't actually explain it.

An AI can skip all of those steps and produce a competent-looking paper in seconds.
But you won't have learned any of the things the assignment was designed to teach.
The paper isn't the point — the process is.

## When in Doubt, Ask

Different professors have different expectations about AI use.
Some may allow AI-assisted outlining; others may not.
Some may require you to disclose any AI use; others may have specific tools they've approved or prohibited.

If you're unsure whether a specific use of AI is acceptable for your assignment, **ask your professor before you submit.**
A quick question before the deadline is always better than a difficult conversation after it.
