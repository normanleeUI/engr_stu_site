# IEEE AI Policy & U of I Resources

As AI tools become more common in academic and professional writing, the organizations that publish engineering research have started setting clear rules about how AI can and cannot be used.
IEEE — the professional association whose citation format you're using for your paper — has published specific policies on AI-generated content.
At the same time, the University of Idaho has partnered with Google to give you access to AI tools designed for learning.
This page covers both so you know where the boundaries are and what resources are available to you.

## IEEE's Position on AI-Generated Content

IEEE's policy on AI-generated content comes down to three key principles:

- **Authors are responsible for all content.**
Every claim, sentence, and citation in a submission must be verified by the human authors.
If an AI tool produces something inaccurate and you include it in your paper, that's your responsibility — not the tool's.

- **AI-generated text must be disclosed.**
If you use AI tools in any part of your writing or research process, you must identify which tools you used and how you used them.
This applies to drafting, paraphrasing, code generation, and figure creation.
IEEE recommends disclosure even for basic editing and grammar tools, though it does not strictly require it in all cases.

- **AI cannot be listed as an author.**
Authorship in IEEE publications requires accountability — the ability to take responsibility for the work, respond to questions, and stand behind the results.
An AI tool cannot do any of those things, so it cannot be an author.

These principles reflect a straightforward idea: the purpose of a published paper is to communicate *your* research and *your* understanding.
AI can be part of the process, but it cannot replace the human judgment that gives a paper its value.

## What This Means for Your Paper

You might be wondering how IEEE's professional publishing rules connect to a two-page class assignment.
The connection is more direct than you'd think.

Your professor is asking you to practice the same skills that working engineers use when they publish: finding credible sources, evaluating evidence, and presenting your own analysis.
IEEE's disclosure requirement exists because readers need to know what the author actually did versus what a tool generated.
The same logic applies to your assignment — your professor needs to see *your* work.

In practical terms, this means:

- If you use an AI tool to help you understand a concept, rephrase an awkward sentence, or check your grammar, that's generally fine — but note it if your professor or syllabus requires disclosure.
- If an AI tool generates a paragraph, a summary of a source, or a section of your paper, that crosses the line from assistance to authorship, even if you edit the output afterward.
- When in doubt, follow IEEE's lead: **disclose what you used and how you used it.**
Transparency is always the safer choice.

## University of Idaho's Google AI Partnership

The University of Idaho has partnered with Google to provide students with access to **Google Gemini**, an AI assistant available through your university Google account.
This partnership is designed to support learning — not to replace the work you do for your classes.

Here's what the tool can help with:

- **Self-paced tutoring.**
If you're struggling with a concept from one of your sources — say, a signal processing technique or a circuit design methodology — you can ask Gemini to explain it in different ways until it clicks.
This is similar to asking a TA for help, except it's available any time.

- **Comprehension checking.**
After you've read a source, you can test your understanding by asking Gemini questions about the material.
If you can't explain the key ideas back, that's a signal you need to reread before writing about it.

- **Writing feedback.**
You can paste a sentence or paragraph you've written and ask for suggestions on clarity or grammar.
The key is that *you* wrote the original text and *you* decide whether to accept the suggestion.

To access Gemini, sign in with your University of Idaho Google account (your `@uidaho.edu` email).
The university's [AI resources page](https://ai.uidaho.edu/) has details on what's available and any usage guidelines.

## Using AI Tools Responsibly

Between IEEE's policies and the university's AI resources, the message is consistent: **AI is a tool for learning, not a shortcut around it.**

Think of it this way — a calculator doesn't make you a mathematician, but it can help you check your work or explore a problem faster.
AI tools work the same way in the research writing process.
They're most valuable when you already have a foundation of understanding and use them to deepen or refine it.

For your two-page IEEE-format paper, a responsible approach looks like this:

1. Do your own research using library databases and search tools.
2. Read and evaluate your sources yourself.
3. Write your paper in your own words, based on your own analysis.
4. Use AI tools for support tasks — understanding a concept, checking grammar, improving a sentence you've already drafted.
5. Disclose any AI assistance per your professor's instructions and IEEE guidelines.

## AI Policies Are Evolving

One important caveat: **AI policies are changing rapidly** across universities, publishers, and professional organizations.
IEEE has updated its guidelines as AI capabilities have expanded, and may revise them again as the technology and its uses evolve.
The University of Idaho's AI resources and policies are also being developed and refined.

Before you submit your paper, check for the latest guidance from both your professor and IEEE.
What's acceptable today may be more restricted — or more permissive — by the time you're working on your next assignment.
